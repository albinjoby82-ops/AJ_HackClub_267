# Connectors and Crimping

A good connector makes a project easier to assemble, test and repair. A poor connection creates intermittent faults, heat and reversed polarity.

## Choose a connector

Consider:

- Number of contacts
- Current and voltage
- Wire size
- Locking and polarisation
- Number of mating cycles
- Strain and vibration
- Availability of the correct crimp tool

Common families include pin headers, JST-PH/XH, screw terminals, USB, barrel connectors, banana plugs and BNC. “JST” is a manufacturer, not one universal connector type.

## Crimp anatomy

A typical open-barrel crimp has:

- Conductor wings gripping bare copper
- Insulation wings providing strain relief
- A locking tang holding the contact inside the housing

## Make a crimp

1. Confirm contact, housing, wire size and tool are compatible.
2. Strip only the specified length.
3. Keep all conductor strands together.
4. Place the contact correctly in the die.
5. Insert the wire until conductor and insulation sit in their wings.
6. Complete the crimp fully.
7. Inspect it before inserting it into the housing.
8. Insert until the locking tang clicks.
9. Perform a gentle tug test.

Do not fill a poor crimp with solder. Solder can wick into the flexible wire and create a new stress point.

## Inspection

- Conductor wings wrap around copper, not insulation.
- Insulation wings support the cable without cutting it.
- No loose strands are visible.
- Contact faces the correct direction in the housing.
- Polarity matches the mating connector.
- No exposed copper can short adjacent contacts.

{: .tip}
> Label both ends before a cable becomes part of a larger harness.
