01 · Origins

A challenge that has been unsolved-then-solved for fifty years

In 1977 IEEE Spectrum announced the Amazing Micro-Mouse Maze Contest: build a self-contained robot that finds its own way to the centre of a maze it has never seen. Around six thousand people registered an interest. When the contest actually ran in New York in 1979, fifteen mice turned up, and the machine that won — Moonlight Flash — was a plain wall follower that got lucky with the maze it was given.

That embarrassment is the reason every maze since has been built the way it is. Within a year the contest had crossed the Atlantic to London and been taken back to Tokyo, where the first All Japan Micromouse Contest ran in November 1980 and not one mouse reached the centre. The rules hardened, the mazes got nastier, and the sport turned into what it is now: a fifty-year-old benchmark problem that people still shave milliseconds off every year.

The numbers at the top end are genuinely absurd. The most-cited benchmark run is Ng Beng Kiat's 3.921 seconds at the 32nd All Japan contest in 2011; current machines are quoted at road speeds around 7 m/s with cornering loads past 6 g, held down by on-board suction fans. You are not going to do that in six hours. You do not need to. You need to understand why those numbers are possible, because the same ideas at one tenth the speed win a one-day contest.

02 · The arena

The maze is a fixed, published standard. Design to the millimetre.

We run to the same classic 16 x 16 standard as the UK Micromouse and Robotics Society (UKMARS). This matters more than it sounds: it means every maze file, every simulator, every tuning value and every write-up from forty years of the sport applies directly to the maze you will be standing in front of. Nothing here is bespoke to Dublin.

Classic micromouse maze specification
Grid16 x 16 cells, 256 cells total
Cell pitch180 mm centre to centre, so the maze is 2880 mm square
Wall thickness12 mm, leaving 168 mm of clear passage between two walls
Wall height50 mm
FinishWhite wall sides, red wall tops, matt (non-gloss) black floor
Posts12 x 12 mm at every lattice point, including where no wall is fitted
StartA corner cell, walled on three sides, so the exit direction is unambiguous
GoalThe 2 x 2 block of cells at the centre of the maze
Outer boundaryFully walled — the maze is closed

Read those numbers as engineering constraints

They are not trivia. They are the spec your mouse is designed against, and each one has a direct consequence:

  • 168 mm of clear passage. A 90 mm-wide mouse leaves 39 mm of clearance either side. That sounds generous until you are travelling a metre per second with a heading error of two degrees. Narrow is forgiving; wide is fast to build but punishing to steer.
  • 50 mm wall height. Your side sensors have to see a wall reliably at that height and see nothing when the wall is absent. Mount them so the beam lands on the wall face, not over the top of it and not down into the floor.
  • Matt black floor, white walls, red tops. That contrast exists so optical sensors have something to work with. It also means a mouse tuned under one lighting rig can read differently under another — calibrate in the room you are racing in.
  • Posts at every lattice point. Even a corridor with no side walls has posts at the cell corners. These give you a periodic 180 mm feature you can use to correct your distance estimate — and they will also produce sensor spikes you must not mistake for walls.
  • The goal is a 2 x 2 block, not a point. Four cells count as "arrived", and the block has exactly one entrance. Your search target is a set, not a coordinate. Write it that way from the start.
03 · The machine

What counts as a micromouse

The constraints on the robot are deliberately loose. There is no weight limit, no cost cap and no height limit. What the rules protect is the spirit of the problem: the mouse solves the maze itself, on board, without cheating the geometry.

  1. It must fit inside a 25 cm square

    Measured on the floor, at all times — you cannot start compact and unfold. There is no height restriction. In practice competitive mice are far smaller than the limit, because size costs you cornering radius, not because the rule forces it.

  2. It must be autonomous and self-contained

    No remote control, no tethers, no off-board computation, no human input once the run starts. Everything the mouse knows, it works out itself from its own sensors.

  3. It must stay on the floor and use the openings

    No flying, jumping or climbing over walls. Combustion and rocket propulsion are out. The maze is to be solved by driving through it.

  4. It must not damage or mark the maze

    No scratching, cutting, burning or marking walls, and nothing may be deposited in the maze. Leaving a trail to navigate by is the oldest disallowed trick in the sport.

  5. It must not shed parts

    The mouse that finishes is the mouse that started. Anything that falls off is a problem for you and for the team running after you.

The rule that actually shapes your design is the one nobody writes down: the maze is unseen. You cannot hard-code a route, pre-load a map, or tune to a layout. Every line of code you write has to work on a maze that is handed to you cold.

04 · The rules of a run

How a run is timed and scored

This is the part teams skim, and it is the part that decides contests. Under the UKMARS classic rules the shape of your slot is fixed, and it rewards a very specific kind of behaviour.

Your slot

  • You get ten minutes in the maze — reducible to seven or fewer by the judges if the entry list demands it — and within that, up to five runs.
  • A run is timed from the mouse leaving the start cell to the mouse reaching the goal. Time spent between runs, thinking, or returning to the start is not part of a run time, but it absolutely is part of your ten minutes.
  • Your score is your best single run. The slow exploratory first run does not drag your result down. This is the single most important fact on this page.

Touching the mouse

You may not touch a running mouse unless a judge tells you to. If the mouse has clearly malfunctioned or cannot continue, the judge will allow a manual recovery. You can also request a recovery in other circumstances — but the price is that all of the mouse's memory of the maze is erased, and it starts again knowing nothing.

Where a touch is permitted and the run continues, expect a touch penalty of three seconds plus one tenth of the run time added to that run.

Work out what that means before you write any code. Five runs, best time counts, and a wipe if you ask for help at the wrong moment. The optimal play is not "go as fast as possible". It is: explore, bank a safe finish, then escalate speed run by run until either the clock or the mouse gives out. A team with one recorded 45-second run beats a team with four spectacular crashes and no time on the board.

05 · Our format

Standard maze. Very non-standard build window.

Most Micromouse entries represent months of work. The Dublin Micromouse Open compresses the whole thing into one Saturday: six hours, one identical kit per team, design, build, program and race on the day. The maze standard is the classic one described above. The build sprint is the competition.

Every team gets the same box: an ESP32, encoded motors, a motor driver, an IMU, distance sensors, a battery management system, a buck converter and a battery pack. The kit is modular deliberately — there is no single correct mouse, and the same parts will walk out of the room as very different machines.

Judging is the fastest verified run to the centre, with further awards for design and reliability. Full-size and mini practice mazes are open all day, and one of the mini mazes is built so that the fastest route cuts diagonally through it. A mouse that only knows how to follow the grid will finish that maze. A mouse that can reason about the path will beat it.

Everything on this page is written to be useful on that day: the theory is the general theory of the sport, and the timings assume you are starting from an empty bench at nine in the morning.

06 · Strategy

Micromouse is two different races wearing one costume

Almost every mouse that has ever won splits the problem in half, because the two halves have opposite objectives.

The search run

The mouse leaves the start knowing nothing except the maze dimensions and where the goal is. It drives carefully, reads walls, builds a map, and reaches the centre. Speed is not the objective here — information is. A search run that arrives with a complete, correct map of the useful part of the maze is worth more than one that arrives four seconds sooner with gaps in it.

The speed run

The mouse returns to the start, computes the best route through the map it now has, and drives that route flat out with no exploration and no hesitation. This is the run that gets timed seriously. It can afford aggression precisely because it already knows every wall on the route.

With five runs available, the standard ladder looks like this:

  • Run 1 — search. Slow, careful, map-building. Reach the centre.
  • Run 2 — conservative speed run. Bank a time. You now have a result no matter what happens next.
  • Run 3 — faster, or a short re-exploration if you suspect a better route exists in cells you never visited.
  • Runs 4–5 — escalate until it breaks. Now a crash costs you nothing but pride.

The ladder is the whole strategy. Because only your best run counts, risk is cheap after you have a time on the board and ruinously expensive before it. Teams lose this contest by trying to win it on run one.

07 · The trap

Why the obvious algorithm cannot work

The first idea everyone has is the left-hand rule: keep your left hand on the wall and walk. It is elegant, it needs almost no memory, and on a maze drawn on paper it genuinely works.

It works on a simply-connected maze — one where every wall is ultimately joined to the outer boundary. Trace a connected wall surface and you must eventually visit everything attached to it.

A contest maze is not built that way. The goal block at the centre is an island: its walls connect to nothing that touches the border. A hand kept on the boundary wall traces the boundary loop, comes back to the start, and does that forever. It never enters the centre, because the centre is not part of the surface it is following.

This is not a theoretical concern; it is the specific reason the modern rules exist. The 1979 contest was won by a wall follower, the organisers concluded the maze had been too kind, and detached centre islands have been standard ever since. If your fallback plan is "at least the wall follower will finish", your fallback plan does not finish.

What you need instead is a mouse that builds a map and searches it. That is a bigger idea, but not a harder one — and there is one algorithm that does it in a form small enough to run on a microcontroller between cells.

08 · The core algorithm

Flood fill: turn the whole maze into a hill, then roll downhill

Flood fill is the algorithm that wins Micromouse, and it has been for decades. The idea is almost insultingly simple.

Label the goal cells 0. Label every cell that is one move from a goal cell 1. Every cell one move from those, 2. Keep going until every reachable cell carries a number: its distance from the goal. That number field is the entire solution.

Now the mouse's decision at every cell collapses to something a microcontroller can do in microseconds: look at the neighbours you can reach, and move to the one with the smallest number. No path finding at run time, no route stored, no backtracking logic. Just descend the gradient. Because every step strictly decreases the distance, you cannot loop, and you cannot fail to arrive.

Under the hood this is a breadth-first search run backwards from the goal, which is what makes it cheap: one flood gives you the optimal move from every cell at once, not just from where you are standing. 

# The flood: breadth-first, outward from the goal cells.
def flood(walls, goal_cells):
    dist = [INFINITY] * 256
    queue = deque()
    for cell in goal_cells:
        dist[cell] = 0
        queue.append(cell)

    while queue:
        cell = queue.popleft()
        for nb in open_neighbours(cell, walls):
            if dist[nb] > dist[cell] + 1:
                dist[nb] = dist[cell] + 1
                queue.append(nb)
    return dist

# The drive: at every cell, take the smallest reachable number.
def next_move(cell, walls, dist):
    return min(open_neighbours(cell, walls), key=lambda nb: dist[nb])

The part that makes it work on a real mouse

The version above assumes you already know the maze. You do not. The mouse discovers walls as it drives, and every new wall can invalidate the numbers.

The naive fix is to re-flood all 256 cells after every single cell you enter. On modern hardware that is genuinely fine — an ESP32 will do a full 256-cell flood in well under a millisecond, and if you are short on time on the day, do exactly this and move on.

The classical refinement, usually called modified or incremental flood fill, only repairs the cells whose distances could actually have changed. When you discover a wall, push the affected cell onto a stack; pop cells and check whether each one is still exactly one more than its cheapest open neighbour; if it is not, correct it and push its neighbours. The correction ripples outward and dies out on its own, usually after touching a handful of cells. That is what lets the update happen while the mouse is still moving, which is the real prize: no stop-and-think pause at every cell boundary.

The optimism rule. Treat every wall you have not yet seen as absent. A cell you have never visited is assumed wide open in all directions. This makes the mouse walk hopefully toward the shortest route that could exist; when it turns out to be blocked, the flood repairs itself and the mouse re-routes. Assume walls are present instead and your mouse becomes a pessimist that explores the whole maze before committing to anything — correct, and far too slow.

09 · Exploration

Reaching the centre is not the same as knowing the maze

Here is the trap that catches good teams. Your search run reaches the goal. You return to the start, flood the map, and drive the best route you know. But the route you know is only the best route through the cells you happened to walk through. The genuinely fastest path may run through cells you never entered, and your map has no idea whether they are open.

The three-stage search

  1. Go to the goal. Flood toward the centre with unknown walls treated as open, and drive the gradient. You will arrive without exploring most of the maze.
  2. Come back to the start. Re-flood with the start cell as the target and drive home. This is free information: the return leg naturally sees walls from the other side, and it usually finds a shorter way back than the way you came.
  3. Go and look at the cells that matter. Not every unknown cell — only the ones that could shorten the route. Flood the map twice: once optimistically (unknowns open) and once pessimistically (unknowns walled). If the two give the same route cost, further exploration cannot help you and you should stop searching and start racing. If the optimistic cost is much lower, the difference is sitting in unexplored cells and it is worth spending clock to go and check them.

That optimistic-versus-pessimistic comparison is the cheapest good decision in the whole contest. It answers "should I explore more or run now?" with a number instead of a feeling, and it costs two floods.

Budget it against the clock, not against curiosity. You have ten minutes total. A search run on a 16 x 16 maze can comfortably eat two or three of them. Decide in advance — in code, not in the moment — how much of your slot goes to exploration before you commit to racing what you have.

10 · The cost function

Your flood counts cells. The clock counts seconds. Those are different.

A plain flood fill finds the path with the fewest cells. That is not the path with the lowest time, and once your mouse is quick the gap between the two becomes the whole margin of victory.

A straight is nearly free: you are already at speed and you stay at speed. A turn is expensive: you decelerate into it, rotate or arc through it, and accelerate out. Every corner is a bite out of your average speed. So a staircase of eleven cells with ten turns and a straight-then-turn route of eleven cells with one turn look identical to a naive flood, and are seconds apart on the floor.

Weighting the flood

The fix is to flood over time instead of cell count. Give each move a cost that reflects what it actually does to your speed: 

# Cost a move by what it does to the clock, not by counting squares.
STRAIGHT      = 1.0   # continue in the current heading
TURN_90       = 3.5   # decelerate, corner, accelerate again

def move_cost(heading_in, heading_out):
    return STRAIGHT if heading_in == heading_out else STRAIGHT + TURN_90

The moment cost depends on which direction you arrived from, a cell is no longer a single node. Model each cell as four states — one per heading — and flood over those 1024 states instead of 256 cells. It is still a breadth-first flood; it is still trivial for an ESP32; and it now produces routes that are actually fast rather than merely short.

Get TURN_90 from measurement, not from taste. Time your mouse over ten straight cells, then over ten cells with a turn in each, and let the difference set the number. A mouse with a slow pivot turn should be routed almost entirely along straights; a mouse with good arc turns can afford corners and should be told so.

11 · The big win

Diagonals: stop driving cell to cell

Everything so far assumes the mouse drives along cell centres and turns in 90° steps. Nothing in the rules says it has to. The maze is a grid of walls; it is not a set of rails.

A staircase of alternating left-right turns is really a straight line at 45°, and a mouse that can recognise that pattern and drive it as one continuous diagonal gets two enormous wins at once. The distance drops by a factor of √2 — about 29% less ground — and, far more importantly, ten decelerate-corner-accelerate cycles collapse into one entry turn, one straight diagonal and one exit turn.

This is where serious mice separate from good ones, and it is exactly why one of the mini practice mazes on the day is built so the fastest line cuts through it diagonally. That maze is not decoration. It is a test of whether your mouse follows the grid or reasons about the path.

How to implement it without rewriting everything

  • Keep the flood on the orthogonal grid. Do not try to make the search itself diagonal. Solve for the route in cells as before.
  • Turn the route into a string of moves — a sequence like F F L R L R F R F.
  • Pattern-match the string. Any alternating L R L R run is a diagonal. Rewrite it as "enter diagonal, travel n half-cells, exit diagonal". Runs of F F F become one long acceleration instead of several short ones.
  • Then hand the rewritten string to the motion controller. Your route planner never has to know what a diagonal is; only the driver does.

Be honest about the cost. On a diagonal your mouse sits at 45° between posts, with much less wall to see and much less room either side — the effective clearance is far tighter than the 168 mm you get in a corridor. Diagonals demand good heading control and a narrow mouse. Build the orthogonal speed run first, get it reliable, and add diagonals as an upgrade when you have a banked time to protect.

12 · Control

The algorithm is the easy half

Flood fill is a page of code and it is correct on the first try. What separates finishing mice from crashing ones is everything underneath it: making the robot actually go where the algorithm said, and knowing where it is while it does.

Motion profiles, not "drive for 400 ms"

Do not command motion in time. Command it in distance, with a velocity profile: accelerate at a fixed rate to a cruise speed, hold, then decelerate so that you arrive at the target distance with your speed at (or near) zero. This trapezoidal profile is the standard tool and it buys you two things — repeatability, and the ability to change your top speed between runs by editing one constant.

That last point is how you climb the ladder from section 06. A conservative banked run and a flat-out final run should be the same code with a different cruise speed and acceleration, not two different programs written under time pressure.

Pivot turns versus arc turns

The simplest 90° turn stops the mouse, rotates it on the spot, and drives on. It is easy, it is accurate, and it is slow: every corner costs you a full stop and a full restart. The faster approach is a smooth arc that carries speed through the bend.

Build the pivot turn first — it is your reliable fallback and it will get you a finish. Then characterise an arc turn as a fixed, tested manoeuvre: a known entry offset, a known radius, a known exit offset. Store those numbers, verify them on the practice maze, and let the route planner call them by name. Do not compute turn geometry on the fly during a run.

Knowing where you are

Your position estimate comes from wheel encoders, and it is wrong. Wheels slip, tyre diameter is not exactly what you measured, and the two wheels do not travel identically. Over a few cells that error is invisible; over a 40-cell speed run it will put you into a wall. You need three corrections working together:

  • Heading from the IMU. Integrating the gyro's rate gives you a far better heading than differencing two encoders. It drifts, so calibrate the zero-rate offset with the mouse absolutely still at power-up, and re-zero whenever you are legitimately stationary. Never calibrate a gyro while someone is nudging the bench.
  • Lateral position from the side walls. Run a PID loop on the difference between your left and right distance readings to stay centred in the corridor. This is your highest-value control loop — and note that it silently stops working the moment a wall disappears, so detect that case and hold your heading instead of steering into the gap.
  • Longitudinal position from the front wall. When you can see a wall ahead, you know exactly how far you are into the cell. Use it to reset your distance-travelled estimate outright. Every front wall is a free re-zero of accumulated encoder error, and a mouse that uses them can run much longer routes without losing itself.

Sensor discipline. Sample your wall sensors at a fixed rate in a timer, not "whenever the loop gets round to it". Decide wall-present versus wall-absent with hysteresis — two different thresholds — so a marginal reading cannot chatter between the two. And read walls at a consistent point in the cell: a reading taken while passing a post is not the same measurement as one taken mid-cell, and mixing them is how phantom walls get into your map. A single false wall in the map will send an otherwise perfect mouse confidently into a dead end.

13 · Hardware

Decisions you make in hour one that you cannot undo in hour five

You get the same parts as everyone else. What differs is how you arrange them, and a few of those choices are effectively permanent once the mouse is soldered.

Geometry

Put the wheel axis under the centre of rotation

If the mouse rotates about a point between its wheels, a turn is a turn. If the mass hangs well behind or ahead of that axis, every turn is also a translation, and your position estimate quietly diverges every time you corner.

Mass

Light beats powerful

Acceleration, braking and cornering all improve with less mass, and none of them cost anything to implement. The battery is usually the heaviest single item — where you mount it is a handling decision, not a packaging one.

Width

Narrow is not just about fitting

The 168 mm passage will accept a wide mouse comfortably. Diagonals will not. Every millimetre you save is margin you get back when you start cutting corners.

Sensors

Angle them, do not just point them

Side sensors angled slightly forward see the wall ahead of the wheels, which gives the steering loop a lead on the error instead of reacting to it late. Aim them at the wall face at mid-height, and check they cannot see the floor or clip over the top.

Power

Budget for sag, not for nominal

A battery under hard acceleration is not at its resting voltage. If your control loops are tuned at full charge they will behave differently at the end of a ten-minute slot. That is what the buck converter in the kit is for — give the logic a stable rail and keep motor transients off it.

Traction

Grip is the real speed limit

Past a certain point the motors are not the constraint — friction is. This is why the fastest mice in the world carry suction fans that press themselves into the floor. You will not build one on the day, but it explains why your mouse stops improving when you raise the acceleration constant.

14 · On the day

A six-hour playbook

Six hours is less than it sounds. The single most common way to lose this event is to spend five hours building something ambitious and forty minutes discovering it does not drive straight. Work in the order below and you will always have a mouse that works, only progressively faster.

  1. Hour 0 – 1 · Move something

    Power up, flash a blink, and get both motors turning under command. Confirm encoder counts change in the right direction for each wheel. Do not build a chassis around a motor you have not spun. Split the team here: one person on mechanics, one on motion, one on sensors.

  2. Hour 1 – 2 · Drive one cell

    Get the mouse to travel exactly 180 mm and stop, and to turn exactly 90° and stop. Repeat each ten times and measure the drift. This hour is unglamorous and it is the whole contest — every later capability is built on these two primitives being trustworthy.

  3. Hour 2 – 3 · See walls

    Read the distance sensors, print raw values, and set your wall-present thresholds against the actual practice maze under the actual room lights. Add hysteresis. Then get the wall-centring loop steering the mouse down a corridor without touching either side.

  4. Hour 3 – 4 · Solve the maze slowly

    Wire the flood fill to the movement primitives and let it search a practice maze at a deliberately embarrassing speed. When it reaches the centre, you have a competition-legal mouse. Everything from here is optimisation, and you should feel much calmer than you did an hour ago.

  5. Hour 4 – 5 · Return, re-run, speed up

    Add the return-to-start leg and the speed run over the stored map. Then raise cruise speed and acceleration in steps, testing after each change, until the mouse starts making mistakes — and back off one step. Add the turn cost weighting if you have the map working cleanly.

  6. Hour 5 – 6 · Freeze and rehearse

    Stop writing features. Charge the battery. Run the full sequence end to end — start button, search, return, speed run — at least twice, exactly as you will on the maze. Decide now, as a team, which speed setting is your banked run and which is your gamble. Write it down. You will not think clearly with a judge's stopwatch running.

The rule that beats every other piece of advice on this page: a mouse that reliably reaches the centre in 60 seconds beats a mouse that would have done it in 12 and didn't. Build for a finish first. Optimise second. Always have a working version you can go back to.

15 · Pre-mortem

The ways mice actually fail

None of these are exotic. Every one of them will happen to somebody in the room on the day. Reading them now costs you two minutes; finding them at hour five costs you the contest.

  • A phantom wall in the map. One bad sensor reading records a wall that is not there. The mouse is now confidently solving a different maze. Guard the map: only record a wall from a reading taken at a consistent position in the cell.
  • Accumulated heading error. The mouse is 3° off after ten cells and clips a post. Correct heading against the walls continuously; do not trust the gyro alone over a long run.
  • The corridor with no side walls. The wall-centring PID loses its reference and steers hard toward the missing wall. Detect "no wall on this side" explicitly and fall back to holding heading.
  • Starting from the wrong place. The start cell is walled on three sides for a reason. If your code assumes the mouse starts centred and facing the opening, make sure a human placing it in a hurry cannot get that wrong.
  • Flat battery mid-slot. Ten minutes of runs, motors stalling against walls, and a pack that was never fully charged. Charge between practice sessions, and know what your mouse does as voltage falls.
  • The un-tested return leg. Teams test "drive to the centre" thoroughly and the trip home not at all — then discover on the maze that the mouse has no idea how to turn around inside the goal block.
  • Asking for a recovery at the wrong moment. Remember that a recovery requested outside a clear malfunction can cost you the mouse's entire memory of the maze. Know before you raise your hand whether you are willing to pay that.
